# How Does a Stock Exchange Work?

## Why this matters
**A stock exchange is an organized marketplace where buyers and sellers submit orders, and a matching engine pairs them together at an agreed price.** Price doesn't just happen — it's the output of a specific mechanical process. Once you understand how exchanges match orders, you understand why prices move in certain patterns, why liquidity matters, and what Delta One's strategy is actually exploiting.

It's easy to think of "the market" as one abstract thing that decides prices. It isn't. It's a very literal, very fast piece of infrastructure processing a firehose of individual orders, and almost everything you'll learn about price behavior traces back to how that infrastructure actually works.

## How it works
Every exchange runs an order book — a live list of all the buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks) sitting unfilled. When you place a market order, you're agreeing to trade at whatever the best available price is right now. When you place a limit order, you're saying you'll only trade at a specific price — and your order sits in the book until someone meets it.

The gap between the best bid and the best ask is called the spread. On liquid stocks like NVDA or META, this spread is fractions of a cent. On thinly traded stocks, it can be dollars wide. That spread is the friction cost of every trade — it's effectively what you pay for the convenience of trading instantly instead of waiting for someone to meet your price.

When a large order hits — say, an institution selling 500,000 shares — it doesn't just fill at one price. It eats through every bid in the book until it's fully filled. Each level it consumes pushes the price lower. This is called market impact, and it's one of the core reasons big players move differently from retail traders: a retail order for 100 shares barely dents the book, but a large institutional order can visibly move the price just by existing.

That's also why institutions often break large orders into many smaller ones, spread out over minutes or hours — trying to hide their true size so they don't move the price against themselves before they've finished trading.

> **Key concept:** Price is not set by any single person or entity — it's the output of millions of competing orders, cleared by a matching engine in microseconds.

## What this looks like in the market
The NYSE and NASDAQ handle billions of shares daily. NVDA alone regularly trades tens of millions of shares on an average day.

That volume means the order book is deep — large trades can be absorbed without moving the price dramatically. This depth is exactly what makes NVDA a viable symbol for sweep-style strategies: there's enough institutional participation to create clean, readable liquidity levels instead of the erratic, gappy price action you'd see on a thinly traded stock.

## Common mistake
Beginners assume the price they see on a chart is *the* price of the stock. It's actually the price of the *last* trade. The real price — what you'd actually get if you traded right now — depends entirely on the current state of the order book, which changes every millisecond.

[[reveal prompt="If the chart shows the last trade, why does that matter for a beginner trying to buy or sell?"]]
Because between the moment you see a price and the moment your order actually fills, the book may have already moved — especially on a fast-moving or thin stock. A market order doesn't guarantee you get the price on the screen; it guarantees you get the *best currently available* price, which can be meaningfully different a second later. This is exactly why large orders get "chewed through" multiple price levels instead of filling at one clean number.
