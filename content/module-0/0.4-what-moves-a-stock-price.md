# What Moves a Stock Price?

## Why this matters
**Stock prices move when the balance between buying pressure and selling pressure shifts — and that shift is driven by changing beliefs about a company's future value.** If you don't understand what moves prices, you're just watching a line go up and down. Understanding the forces behind price action is what separates a trader who can reason about the market from one who just reacts to it. Everything Delta One does is built on understanding these forces at the institutional level.

Most beginners start by memorizing chart patterns without ever asking what actually causes the pattern in the first place. This lesson is the "why" underneath the "what" — once you have it, patterns stop looking like magic shapes and start looking like the visible footprint of real buying and selling.

## How it works
At the most mechanical level, prices move because of order flow imbalance. If more buy orders arrive than sell orders at the current price, sellers can charge more — the price rises. If more sell orders arrive, buyers can demand a discount — the price falls. This is the same order-book mechanic from the last lesson; everything else in this lesson is really just "what causes the imbalance."

But what drives those orders? At the retail level: news, earnings, analyst upgrades, hype, fear. At the institutional level: macro positioning, sector rotation, options hedging, and systematic fund flows. The institutional layer dwarfs retail — a single large fund rebalancing its portfolio can move a mid-cap stock by several percent with no news at all, simply because their order size is large relative to that stock's typical volume.

Macro events hit prices differently. An interest rate decision from the Fed doesn't change NVDA's products or customers — but it changes the discount rate used to value future earnings, which changes what every institution is willing to pay for every stock simultaneously. That's why macro days can move markets with no company-specific news: the entire market is repricing the same underlying assumption at once.

Time horizon matters too. A day trader reacts to order flow imbalance measured in seconds. A pension fund rebalancing its portfolio might be responding to a shift that plays out over a quarter. Both are "moving the price" — just on completely different clocks, which is part of why the same stock can look calm on a weekly chart and chaotic on a one-minute chart at the same time.

> **Key concept:** Most retail-visible price moves (earnings, news) are just the visible surface. The deeper driver is institutional order flow — large players repositioning their portfolios based on changing risk and return calculations.

## What this looks like in the market
In late 2022, META's stock fell from $380 to $88 — a 77% drop.

The company was still profitable. The fall was driven by rising interest rates (making future earnings worth less), a slowdown in ad revenue growth, and the market repricing the risk of Zuckerberg's metaverse bet. Three institutional-level factors, no product failure.

Then in 2023, META's "year of efficiency" triggered one of the largest single-year recoveries in large-cap history. The product didn't change. The market's belief about future earnings and management discipline did — the same company, radically re-rated twice in two years, with no change to what it actually built or sold.

## Common mistake
Beginners look for *a reason* for every price move. Most intraday moves don't have a news catalyst — they're the result of institutional order flow, options market maker hedging, or systematic strategy triggers. Trying to explain every candle with a headline will mislead you.

[[reveal prompt="If most price moves have no clear news reason, why do financial news sites always seem to have an explanation ready anyway?"]]
Because a plausible-sounding explanation is easy to write after the fact, and readers expect one. A financial writer can point to *some* headline from that day and connect it to the move — even if the actual cause was an unrelated options expiration, a large fund rebalancing, or simple order flow imbalance. The explanation feels satisfying; it isn't necessarily correct.
